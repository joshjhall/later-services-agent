Framing

The problem, tested assumptions, and the autonomy split

Source: docs/discovery/framing.md

The initial thesis, organized and tested against public research and what Later leadership said during the interview process (labelled Interview). Sources live in research.md and ../assumptions.md; the workflow diagrams live in ../flows.md. Verdicts: ✅ holds, 🔶 holds with a correction, ❌ does not hold as stated.

1The thesis in one paragraph

A Services campaign manager runs a two-sided matchmaking business (our framing, not Later's): brands bring business objectives, creators bring audiences, and the human's job is to make good matches and then deliver them — on time, on brief, on budget — while keeping both sides' trust. Most of the day is lost to coordination (chasing, tracking, status updates), not judgment. The agent should absorb the coordination, prepare the judgment calls so they take seconds instead of minutes, and escalate the moments that genuinely need a human voice. The interface is a state-driven workspace (what needs me, why, and what I need to decide it), not a chat window.

Where this fits Later's direction

Later leadership describes the target as an agentic factory. Agents do the work, people observe and govern, and when something goes wrong a person tunes the robot rather than redoing the job. Brands get a polished front desk and never see the machinery (Interview). That gives this work three anchors:

  1. Design time and run time. The planned conductor agent first designs a campaign (its output is a "bill of materials") and then runs it (Interview). The brief's three moments split the same way. Stating the goal and reviewing the lineup are design time: the CM approves a plan before anything is spent or sent. The hard moment is run time: the plan meets reality, and the agent escalates the exception. These need two different, reusable review patterns: approve a plan and decide an exception. They are the strongest candidates to standardize across a multi-agent product (§7). For the AM, run time is two phases, so the campaign has three: Plan → Make → Run. Make is producing the assets: briefs, routine nudges, drafts, approvals. The quiet creator lives here. Run is after the content is live: going live, amplifying, measuring against KPIs. They involve different decisions, risks and people. Make is coordination-heavy; Run is money and measurement. The same two review patterns cover both. The campaign screen uses the same three phases (../information-architecture.md §4), with Run split into Measure and Amplify.
  2. The CM as a factory supervisor, not an operator. The CM's day moves from doing the steps to approving plans, deciding exceptions, and tuning the agent's policy. Corrections that only fix one case don't scale. A correction that changes the agent's policy does.
  3. The back of house stays behind the curtain. Every client-facing artifact (lineup deck, status note, wrap report) goes out under the CM's name. The brand never sees the agent, its confidence levels, or its log.

The business case is cost to serve, measured with outcome quality (on time, on brief, KPI attainment), not efficiency alone (Interview). These inputs are thin: three conversations and public material, with no time observing a Services pod. This is an informed direction to validate, not a specification.

Correction 1: the unit of daily work is smaller than the campaign. It is each creator's progress through the workflow, with the deliverables they owe, plus budget pacing. Later Influence tracks each creator through about a dozen stages in four phases: Candidates (Candidates, Invited, Applied, Deliverables), Participants (Confirmation, Pre-Payment, Draft, Draft Review, Content Creation), Complete (Post Review, Post-Payment, Complete) and Dropped (Public). A campaign of ~100 creators (Interview: the CTO's example; Public: Premier Protein had 78) means about a hundred of these moving at once, each with deliverables that can stall. That is where the coordination load comes from.

Correction 2: it is a pod, not one person. The AM delegates execution to coordinators and quality-checks their work. The agent is a participant in the pod, not a member: it takes on coordination work, but calling it a teammate personifies it in an unhelpful way. Supervising it is also not the same as supervising a coordinator. Agents respond to how they're asked (scoped, well-aimed questions do better than open-ended ones), they're strong at convergent work such as chasing, checking and drafting from a brief, and they're weak at divergent work such as brainstorming. So the AM needs different tools and, over time, different skills. The product should carry some of that skill for them (scoped asks, prepared questions, showing what the agent used) rather than assume it (design position).

3Testing the assumptions

A1Corrected

It's CRM-like: AM → accounts → campaigns → creators/content

Right shape, but pod-based, and the working unit is creator × deliverable × stage (§2).

A2Corrected

The human's core value is matching creators to campaigns

Matching matters, but Later has a dedicated Talent Partnerships casting role and is pushing matching into AI (EdgeAI, Brand Suitability Insights). Public AM job descriptions emphasize client trust, on-time/on-brief delivery, compliance, turning data into insight, renewal/expansion. Reframe: the human's value is judgment and trust on both sides — matching is one expression of it.

A3Doesn't hold

Pivot after ~20% of budget is spent and KPIs lag; AM authorizes the next tranche

No evidence of tranche release or a 20% trigger. Creator fees are mostly committed at contracting (flat fee or 50/50). The documented flex is: (a) a paid amplification reserve used to boost top organic posts within 48–72h (Later's Crumbl and Chobani case studies), (b) swapping or adding creators, (c) scaling top creators and dropping underperformers in the next wave. Later calls this budget pacing. The decision moment is real; the mechanism is different.

A4Corrected

Second side: new creators arrive seeking partnerships and must be vetted

Real, but largely a separate org and product (Creator Operations, the Later Creator app / Mavely, HubSpot). For Services it shows up as (a) creators in a campaign's Applied stage and (b) the pod's roster of known, trusted creators. The creator-side view for a CM is "my bench," not an intake funnel.

A5Holds

Deadline reminders can run on a cron; escalate to a human ~3 days out

Matches recruiting automation ("notify recruiter if no activity in 3 days") and Later's own workback (brief T-14, draft T-7, approval T-3, live T-0). Refinement: escalate against the draft deadline, not go-live, and weight by slack, substitutability, and the creator's history. Missing T-7 is the real alarm.

A6Holds

Showing the right information the right way compresses review (as in Meridian/Tyrell)

Strongly supported. Recruiting agents converged on per-criterion evidence ("met / partly / not met") instead of a single score; HAX G11 ("make clear why") is the most-violated guideline.

A7Holds

A library of state-driven widgets beats fixed pages

Consistent with sales/CS tooling: Gong's tunable deal warnings, HubSpot health bands that create tasks only on band change, Salesforce Next Best Action cards with accept/reject. The model selects and fills curated widgets; it does not generate UI ("Generative UI Is Three Things. Only One Ships.").

A8Holds

Client-centric primary view, creator-centric secondary

AMs are measured per account (renewal, expansion), so client-centric fits. Creator view = roster/bench, with reliability history.

A9Holds

Chat is secondary to analysis, summaries, focused UI

"Chat is a good input primitive but a bad operating environment for agents" (Crosley), and the same argument in "Chat Is an Input, Not an Interface." Keep a command/ask affordance; don't make it the home screen.

A10Corrected

It resembles recruiting more than a marketplace

Holds for matching (job order ≈ brief, talent pool ≈ roster, internal submittal gate ≈ AM review before the brand sees a lineup). Breaks on two points: a placement ends at hire, but a booking starts a production tail (drafts, revisions, compliance, go-live, reporting); and creators are repeat suppliers booked by competing brands, so conflicts and reliability matter more.

4What the thesis is missing

These came out of the research and aren't in the original notes. Several are better "hard moments" than the obvious ones.

  1. Rights are separate, priced, and time-boxed. Usage rights, whitelisting/Spark/Partnership Ads permissions, and exclusivity are negotiated separately. If whitelisting wasn't contracted, the "boost the top post" pivot can't happen. If it expires mid-flight, ads silently stop.
  2. The brand is also a source of delay. Brand review and (in regulated categories) legal review are separate gates with their own SLAs. Most delay comes from feedback loops, not creators. The agent should chase the brand too, politely.
  3. FTC disclosure is ongoing liability. The brand (and therefore the agency) shares liability. Checking disclosure at draft review isn't enough; the live post must be checked too.
  4. Gifting ≠ paid. Seeded product carries no posting obligation (post rates ~10–40%). Different expectations, different chasing rules.
  5. Payment is a relationship lever. Late payment is common and creators remember it. The agent should chase internal payment approvals as hard as it chases creators.
  6. Macro creators come through talent managers. Different counterpart, different tone, own approval loop.
  7. Client reporting is a deliverable, not overhead. Status updates, wrap reports and SBRs drive renewal. Drafting them is ideal agent work; the narrative ("why this matters") stays human.
  8. Measurement is contested. EMV is widely criticized (Later still reports it via the Ayzenberg index); promo codes under- and over-count. The KPI framework must be agreed with the client up front, and the agent should not imply false precision.
  9. The workflow has variants, not one path. Product shipped or not; awareness, engagement or affiliate sales (Interview). Milestones and chasing rules change by variant: a gifted, sales-led creator is chased differently from a paid awareness creator. The data model already treats milestones as sets of facts (§6), so a variant adds or removes facts rather than requiring a new workflow.
  10. Creators behave like consumers (Interview). Anything the agent sends to a creator needs consumer-grade tone and timing. A double reminder or a robotic nudge costs goodwill with someone Later will book again.
  11. Draft review already has an AI checker (Interview). The agent should consume it, not rebuild it. The open question is the daily-use UX, and Later leadership has a view: summarize the findings and send them to the creator, rather than confirm every check.
  12. Competitors' agents all live at the front of the funnel (discovery, outreach, shortlists). The middle of the campaign — deliverable tracking, brief compliance with evidence, live-post QA, feedback consolidation — is still manual everywhere. That is exactly what the exercise asks about, and it is where Later Services can differentiate.

5Who does what: the autonomy split

Later describes each job moving along a maturity curve: manual → software-supported → AI-assisted (the human drives) → agentic (the agent starts; the human curates, governs and applies taste) (Interview). 6Order independence and concurrent updates

Most of what a campaign needs can arrive in any order, from several people at once. Ana's rate, her usage-rights scope, her shipping address and her disclosure acknowledgement can land in any sequence. The CM, a coordinator, the agent and the brand may each touch the same partnership in the same hour, often as part of larger batches that overlap: the CM approves a lineup that includes Ana while a coordinator confirms her offer and the agent's daily run sends her a reminder. The system has to give the same result regardless of order or repetition, and it has to surface real disagreements instead of letting the last writer win. This applies "Idempotent Design: When Order Shouldn't Matter."

Principles

  1. Milestones gate on sets, not sequences. (In the brief this is the workflow contract: declared inputs, collected in any order, producing a declared output. What that output changes next is the decision network.) Each milestone (ready to brief, lineup approved, ready to post, ready to pay) is a checklist of facts and decisions. Inside a milestone, collect them in any order; the milestone opens when the set is complete. Any required order that exists only because of the data model (for example "contract before rate entry") is a shadow constraint and should be removed.
  2. Every update is keyed, so repeats are no-ops. A fact is identified by what it is about and what it says, such as Ana / deliverable 2 / brand approval. Recording the same value twice changes nothing. It also leaves an attribution trail, so the second person sees "already approved by Priya, 10 min ago" instead of a duplicate action.
  3. External effects are idempotent by construction. Messages, offers and payments are the irreversible actions. Each carries an idempotency key, such as reminder / deliverable 2 / T-8, so a retried agent run, a re-run schedule or two people clicking "nudge" never double-message a creator or double-pay. A double-sent reminder is the most visible way this design can fail, and a creator notices it immediately.
  4. Writes say what they were based on. An update carries the version its author saw. If the fact has changed since then, the update becomes a conflict card rather than an overwrite. The card shows both values, who made each change, when, and why. Authority (client > CM > coordinator > agent) can settle a conflict automatically only when the rule is explicit and logged. Decisions are never settled silently by timestamp.
  5. Proposals expire when their inputs change. Each agent proposal records the facts it used. If Ana submits her draft while a "swap Ana for Ben" card waits in the queue, the card withdraws itself and says why. A batch approval skips items that are already resolved or stale, and reports what it skipped.
  6. Passing a milestone seals what came before it. Once the lineup is approved and sent to the client, changing a member means explicitly reopening that milestone, which is visible and attributed. Changes after a milestone never mutate earlier decisions quietly.
  7. Partial entries are first-class. An item can be added before it's complete. It shows what it still needs, and the person fills it in when they get to it. A drive-through register that forces size and drink on a "#1" before it will take the "#5" makes the cashier hold the rest of the order in their head. For us: adding Ana and Rio to a lineup doesn't require finishing Ana's terms first. Both are added, and each shows its gaps ("needs rate, rights").
  8. Equivalent inputs give equivalent results. Two paths that describe the same thing produce the same record. A cheeseburger, a medium fry, and a medium Coke ordered separately should be the same order, at the same price, as a medium #1 with a Coke. For us: a standard creator package entered as a bundle, or as its deliverables one at a time, gives the same partnership record and the same fee. The system recognizes the equivalence; the person doesn't have to know which path is "right."

Why this belongs in a UX brief. These are trust mechanics. A CM can only delegate to an agent, and share work across a pod, if repeats are safe and disagreements are visible. "Already handled" and "this changed while you were looking" are the two states this produces in the interface. Both should be first-class widget states.

7Experience concepts

Hierarchy of cognition (as with Reva): what needs me → why → what I need to decide it → do it.

  • Today (cross-account): a sized decision queue, not a feed. Each item is a card with the agent's proposal, its evidence, its confidence, and accept / edit / reject. Ordered by consequence × time-to-deadline, not recency.
  • Account (primary view, e.g. a beverage brand): health of all campaigns against their objectives, budget pacing, what's waiting on the client, what's waiting on us.
  • Campaign: the creator × deliverable board by stage, with named risk warnings (Ghosted, Draft overdue, Brand feedback stalled, Rights expiring, Post missing, Below forecast).
  • Roster (secondary view, creator-centric): the bench. Reliability (on-time rate, revisions, responsiveness), audience fit, past performance, conflicts, relationship notes. No platform exposes a reliability score today; the agent produces this data naturally while chasing.
  • Agent log: what the agent did on its own, with undo. This is how trust is earned and audited.

Two review patterns, used everywhere. Approve a plan (design time: KPI framework, match criteria, lineup) shows the proposal, the criteria it was built against, per-item evidence, and the gate it unlocks. Decide an exception (run time: quiet creator, failed draft check, underperforming post) shows what happened, why it crossed the line, the options with their consequences, and the agent's recommendation. Every card has one primary action, a secondary that may be a prerequisite to it, and labels that mean one thing everywhere (Interview: mixed labels and inverted button hierarchy are a known gap in today's product).

Exceptions first. Draft review shows what failed, with evidence, and a ready-to-send fix request. Passing checks collapse to a count. Reviewing every check is for validating the checker, which is a separate job and a separate surface.

Tune, don't just correct. A rejection carries a reason. When the same kind of correction repeats, the agent proposes a policy change ("escalate quiet creators on this account at T-8, not T-7") for the CM to accept. That makes the CM's judgment durable, and it is how actions move up the maturity curve.

One widget, many states. A campaign widget renders differently depending on what the campaign needs: "approve lineup," "review 4 drafts," "Creator X is quiet, draft due in 2 days," "top post is outperforming, boost?" The widget library is the reusable pattern set, and the strongest answer to the brief's question about standardizing patterns across several agents. It only stays standard if someone owns it as a permanent function ("Owning the Design System").

Layout and navigation are in ../information-architecture.md: top navigation with Find or ask (the command palette), a core context area, and a contextual widget rail. The five ways AI appears in that layout are in ../principles.md §7.

Chat is a command/ask affordance (e.g. "why did you rank her above him?", "draft a note to the client about the delay"), scoped to the current object. It is not the home screen.

8Scoping for the prototype

The brief requires: state the goal, review the proposed lineup, and one hard moment.

  • State the goal → the demand-side flow: objective → KPI framework → constraints. The agent drafts; the CM edits and confirms.
  • Review the lineup → the matching flow: per-criterion evidence, roster rediscovery, conflicts, backups, and the internal gate before anything reaches the brand.
  • Hard moment → a creator goes quiet with a deadline close. It uses the cron-plus-escalation idea, shows the autonomy ladder end to end (agent nudges alone → agent escalates → human calls), and connects to the roster (reliability history) and to the client (do we tell them, and what do we say). The candidate twist: the best fallback is a backup from the approved lineup, which pays off the lineup review earlier in the demo.

Probable cuts (to state in the brief; rank candidate agent tasks with CFS, Commonality × Frequency × Severity, so the cuts are defensible): creator intake funnel (separate org), contracting and payments UI, paid media buying, the full analytics surface, real LLM integration.

9Open questions

  1. How much of the pod does the agent replace versus amplify? (Coordinator workload is the obvious target; say so carefully.)
  2. Does the brand see the agent at all, or only the agency's output? (Settled in direction by the front-desk principle: the brand sees only what the CM puts their name on. Open: whether brands eventually want to see that agents are involved, as a selling point.)
  3. Where is the line for autonomous external messages? Reminders to creators feel safe; anything to the client does not, at first.
  4. How do we show confidence without false precision, especially on performance forecasts?
  5. How does autonomy get earned: per CM, per account, per action type? What's the revocation path after a mistake?
  6. What is the authority order for automatic conflict resolution, and which fact types must never resolve automatically? (Draft answer: money, rights and anything client-facing always go to a human.)
  7. What exactly is in the conductor's "bill of materials," and which parts of it does a CM approve versus only see? (The prototype assumes: KPI framework, match criteria, lineup with backups, workback, and the reserve.)
  8. How should time saved be measured? Cost to serve per campaign, per CM, without on-time and on-brief delivery slipping, is the obvious pair. Measure from when the work actually starts (gathering the brief and chasing inputs), not from when the screen opens.

Principles

How the agent fits into a product people already use

Source: docs/principles.md

The beliefs behind the prototype. Each one is a working position, not a research finding. Framing tests the domain assumptions against public sources, and this page covers the product and interaction philosophy. Everything here may change once there is real user research (§11).

Unless marked otherwise, every claim here is a design position (my own judgement, not Public or Interview). Percentages and examples are Invented.

1Evolve the product in production; don't replace it

Later Influence is already in production, and account pods use it every day. The agent arrives as a series of improvements to the workflows people already know, not as a new product they have to relearn.

  • Change at the pace people can absorb. If we change too much too fast, users get frustrated, work around the tool or abandon it, and the agent never gets the chance to earn trust.
  • The existing UI stays home. Navigation, objects and workflows remain where they are. The first versions add information, remove steps and simplify decisions inside those workflows.
  • New capability is additive and opt-in. Power-user surfaces (command palette, widget rail, chat) are layered on top, so someone who ignores them loses nothing.

2One record, any actor

Anything AI can do (automate, assist, decide, or help someone decide) can also be done by a rule or by a person. All three write the same record in the same place in the UI. The only difference is who did it.

The same entry is shown at three levels of detail, depending on who's looking:

Who's looking What they see as the author Example
Outside the pod (creator, client) The account manager's team, always Email to Ana: from Maya's team
The pod A person's name, or system Draft reminder sent to Ana · system · Called Ana · Jordan
Engineering, product, QA Exactly which rule or agent, its version, the prompt and inputs, and the output rule draft-cadence@v3, T-8 · agent nudge@2026-11-14, prompt #a91f, inputs…
  • Outside the pod, nobody sees the machinery. A creator or client never needs to know whether a person, a rule or an agent sent something. It comes from the account manager's team, and she owns it.
  • Inside the pod, "the system did it" is enough. Most people don't distinguish between a rule and an agent, and the mix will change over time as agent work moves into rules (§3). Showing the distinction would change people's expectations every time it shifts. What matters is whether a person or the system acted.
  • Every actor reads the same history. People and the system all see "last reminder sent 2 days ago," so nobody double-sends. Anti-spam limits (for example, at most one reminder per creator per 48 hours) are enforced on the record itself, regardless of who sends.
  • Nothing is hidden. The system acts on my behalf but never out of sight. I can see what happened, who did it and when, then act on it, undo it where possible, or turn that behaviour off.
  • What has happened can't be edited. Attribution and undo apply where an action is still reversible. Once something has happened outside the system (an email sent, an offer made, money spent), that entry is permanent. The fix is a new action, such as a follow-up, which gets its own entry. That makes the record an audit trail, not just a history.
  • The full trace is what improves the system. Versioned, attributed records are how problems get diagnosed, and how repeated agent behaviour gets found and turned into rules.

3Rules first, agent for the exceptions, human for the ambiguous

Agents are a good way to discover which repetitive work can be automated. Over time most of that work should move into plain rules and small functions, which are cheaper, faster and easier to verify. The agent's long-term job is the exceptions.

Don't use a model where a rule will do, the same way you don't use the largest model when a smaller one is good enough. Rules give the same answer every time; models don't. The full trace in §2 is how repeated agent behaviour gets found and rewritten as code.

Illustrative split, not a measurement. Trust follows from the shape: most outcomes are discrete and reliable, the agent never takes an extreme position, and ambiguity goes to a person instead of being guessed. When every outcome is recorded the same way (§2), people can check that the rules are correct and are implemented correctly, and in my experience most of them get comfortable with that quickly.

4Ask less, so the asks matter; enforce limits in code

Approval only protects anything if people actually read it. If the account manager's job becomes approving everything the agent suggests, approval becomes a reflex: people click "continue" without reading. Coding agents showed this; reducing how often they ask is what made the remaining prompts worth reading. So:

  • New kinds of action start gated, especially messages to creators or clients and spending money.
  • They move to automatic as the record shows they're safe. Routine reminders, small capped tests and low-risk spend should be automatic early, once testing supports it.
  • Escalation paths stay deliberately few, so an escalation means this one is different, and gets read.

Guardrails are rules and code, not prose. Telling a model not to do something in its prompt is not a guardrail: models do things they were explicitly told not to. Limits are enforced outside the model, by deterministic checks it can't argue with:

  • Thresholds and caps: the agent may spend up to $200 per test, capped at $1,000 per campaign per day. Two $200 buys over the cap, or one $20,000 buy, are blocked and escalated. Invented figures.
  • Pattern matching on things that always need a person (legal terms, complaints, client-facing claims).
  • Adversarial review: a separate check, ideally not the same model, reviews what the agent proposes before it goes out.

5Control is contextual

Autonomy is set per action type (autonomy lanes) and can be narrowed for a specific creator, account or situation.

  • A sensitive creator: the agent doesn't message them. It reminds me to get in touch.
  • A creator who prefers phone calls: the agent alerts me by email or text, I make the call, and the call is logged against the same record (automatically, if call capture is available). See flows §6.
  • Any action type: I can lower the agent's autonomy from acts to proposes to off, and the change is logged too.

6Forms first; chat when the input is genuinely unstructured

Conversation is useful, but it is not the main way an account manager works. Most work is filling in a form, writing a message, reviewing copy, and approving or declining. Those stay as direct, traditional UI. Chat is there when the question is open-ended ("compare this account's last three campaigns") and is always reachable, never required. (Background: "Chat Is an Input, Not an Interface," research.)

Most decisions take one of four outcomes. Approving a creator's draft, a lineup member or a proposal comes down to:

  1. Approve, as is.
  2. Approve with conditions: "make these changes."
  3. Decline unless: "we'd go ahead if you change these things."
  4. Decline.

Options 2 and 3 collect the conditions after the choice. This matches Later's own draft review states (Approved / Approved with Corrections / Needs Work) and the shape of rental-application screening. The UI's job is to collect and infer what the person would otherwise have to gather by hand, and then reduce the decision to these few choices.

8Widgets are self-contained and selected, never generated

One fixed library of well-defined widgets serves three places: the rail, answers in a conversation, and eventually the core screens. The rail draws from it like this: In a campaign's context there might be a dozen candidates. The model picks the 2–4 that fit what the data shows, and fills them. As patterns become clear, a deterministic rule takes over that choice (§3 again).

Ambiguous asks get interpreted, and the interpretation is shown. "Compare this asset with her last three" could mean wording, audience and expected outcome, or actual results. If the asset is live, the useful comparison is early performance against her previous posts at the same point, to see whether it's on pattern and predict where it will land. The agent picks a dimension, says which one it picked, and offers the others.

How this could be built (design position; not built). Several specialist agents and skills, orchestrated with something like LangGraph: one interprets the request, one chooses the widget, one fills it. Two emerging protocols fit: A2A for agents handing work to each other, and A2UI (Google), where an agent can only request components from a catalog of approved components the app owns. That's the "selected, never generated" rule, enforced by the protocol.

Example widgets: compare to similar campaigns · suggested next steps · recent activity · next scheduled message (send now or edit) · comparable creatives and what worked with this audience.

Each widget declares the data it accepts. A cost widget might take CPM, cost per click, cost per view or cost per sale, from different sources, and show each in the right form. Which sources and types a widget supports is part of its contract, so the agent can only pick widgets that fit the data available.

Each widget handles two kinds of responsiveness:

  • Space: its own layout from narrow rail to full panel.
  • Data quantity: different designs for 0, 1, about 10, about 100 and 1,000+ items. Comparing against one past campaign is a side-by-side view, against ten it's a ranked list, and against five hundred it's a distribution with the current campaign marked on it. Empty, loading, stale and "already handled" are states too (framing §6).

9Every campaign makes the next one better

Each campaign adds to the record of what worked, for which audience and with which creator. Campaign N+1 starts with everything learned up to N. The roster's reliability data, the benchmarks behind KPI targets and the phrasing suggestions in feedback all come from that record. That compounding is the long-term case for doing this inside Later rather than with a generic tool.

10The agent can run small experiments

Some decisions are better tested than predicted. With a small, capped share of the amplification budget (for example $5–10 of every $100), the agent can run early micro-experiments: boosting a post against a few audiences, keywords or placements to see where the next dollar works best. It combines those results with this client's history and comparable campaigns, then proposes how to split the remaining spend across a small number of buckets (2–5), each with its reasoning and expected outcome.

  • The test budget is set up front by a person, is capped, and is logged like any other spend (§2).
  • The agent proposes the allocation and a person approves it. Spend above the client-approval threshold still goes to the client (flows §7).
  • It only works where whitelisting or partnership-ad rights are in the contract.
  • This moves toward Later's stated ambition of influencer marketing as a programmatic channel (Interview), but one bounded step at a time.

All figures are Invented.

11What this work is not

This is a 2–4 hour exercise about what is really the job itself, which would take years to deliver fully. It is an informed guess at direction, not a researched proposal.

Not done: user interviews, shadowing account pods, heuristic review of the current product, a working session with engineering on how the data is actually organized (the object model in information-architecture.md is mine, not Later's), competitive teardown, analysis of comparable industries (CRM, recruiting, rental screening, and medical or financial matching, where complex rules match two parties across many variables, some objective and some not).

What follows from that: I'm not proposing the quick wins inside today's UI and navigation, although there are certainly many. Those come out of the research. The first 90 days would be research to find where the current workflows lose the most time and accuracy. CFS scoring would then rank what to build first.

2The core objects

Like Reva, the product works around a few objects, and every screen is about one of them.

  • Portfolio is project management across everything. It's the top-level screen for an account manager or anyone else with a book of work.
  • Account is one brand: its campaigns, health, renewal, and what's waiting on the client versus on us.
  • Campaign is one project. It is made of asset slots, the deliverables the plan calls for. Each asset is tied to one creator.
  • Asset is the smallest unit: one deliverable from one creator, through drafts, approval, going live and results.
  • Roster cuts across these. It's the creator-centric view (reliability, history, fit), reached from any asset or lineup.

Over-casting is normal. If a campaign needs 10 assets, the account manager may line up 12–14 creators so there are backups. The model treats a campaign as slots filled by candidates, so a backup is a first-class state and not a workaround. That's also what makes the hard moment's one-click swap possible (flows §6). Invented ratio; ask a pod.

3The portfolio groups by what's needed, not by name

The portfolio is a dashboard organized by state and next need, not an alphabetical list of accounts:

Group Meaning Example

This is the decision queue seen from the portfolio level. Today (in the personas, key paths and prototype) and Portfolio are the same screen: "Today" names the habit (start the day here) and "Portfolio" names the object. Account and campaign screens apply the same grouping to their own scope.

4A campaign has three phases

A campaign screen has three working areas, matching the phases in the brief: Plan → Make → Run. They follow the campaign's lifecycle, but you can open any of them at any time.

Phase Question it answers Contents Later's terms
Plan Are we set up to succeed? Goal and KPIs, match criteria, lineup and backups Design time
Make Are the right assets getting made? Briefs, nudges, drafts, approvals, scheduling Run time, part 1
Run Is it working, and how do we push what works? Measure: KPIs against targets, pacing against forecast, per-asset results, comparable campaigns. Amplify: boosts from the reserve, small tests, next-wave changes Run time, part 2

The campaign's state picks the default tab: Plan until the lineup is approved, Make until the first post is live, Run after that. All three are always one click away. A card can belong to a tab the campaign doesn't open on, for example a quiet creator (Make) on a campaign already in Run. The default tab stays tied to the phase, so the screen is predictable; the tab shows a count of open cards, and every card links its campaign name to the tab that holds it. Open question: whether Amplify belongs to the account manager or to Paid Media.

5The throughline: always know where you are and what's next

  • The breadcrumb is the map. Every screen shows its place in portfolio › account › campaign › asset, and each level is a link.
  • "What's next" goes with you. Each level shows the top item from the queue for that scope. A decision card looks the same on the portfolio, account and campaign screens, and resolving it in one place resolves it everywhere.
  • The rail follows the object. On an asset it shows the creator's history and comparable creatives. On a campaign it shows pacing and recent activity. On the portfolio it shows what the agent did overnight.
  • The palette jumps across levels. It's a shortcut and doesn't replace navigating the hierarchy.

What the paths share

All seven are built from three patterns: the decision queue, approve a plan, and decide an exception. They follow four rules:

  1. One primary action, labeled for the decision it records.
  2. Evidence before recommendation, failures before passes.
  3. Every action names its actor and lands in one log.
  4. New kinds of action that reach a client or spend money start gated, and become automatic as they prove safe. Limits are enforced in code, not in prompts.

These are the patterns proposed for standardizing across agents (design-system-proposal.md).

Flows

How information moves between brand, creators, agent and CM

Source: docs/flows.md

How information moves through the system. Diagrams are Mermaid, so they render on GitHub and in VS Code with bierner.markdown-mermaid. The reasoning behind them is in discovery/framing.md.

The flows split along Later's own design time / run time line (Interview: the planned conductor agent first designs a campaign, then executes it). Flows 2–4 are design time: the agent drafts a plan and the CM approves it before anything is spent or sent. Flows 5–7 are run time: the plan meets reality and the agent escalates exceptions. Flows 8–9 cover both.

Legend used in the swimlane notes: automatic (logged, undoable) · needs approval (the agent proposes, the CM approves) · a person only.

1The system at a glance: two sides meeting at the partnership

The loops matter more than the line. Performance feeds the roster (the roster also informs what performance to expect): every campaign updates what we know about each creator's reliability and results, which makes the next match better. Reporting feeds renewal: the account comes back with the next objective. That second loop is how the AM is measured.

2Demand side: objective to KPI framework (the "state the goal" moment)

Objective Primary KPIs Measurement caveats
Awareness Reach, impressions, video views, CPM Platform-reported; reach is estimated
Consideration Engagement rate, saves, shares, CTR, CPE, sentiment Engagement rate varies hugely by tier
Conversion / affiliate Attributed revenue, codes, affiliate sales and commission (Later Creator), CPA, ROAS Codes leak and undercount the halo effect; agree the method up front
(Reported, contested) EMV Later uses the Ayzenberg index; many clients distrust it

3Supply side: creator intake, vetting, and the roster

Most new-creator intake belongs to Creator Ops, not Services. For a CM, the supply side is mostly the roster: who I trust, who delivers, who performed for whom.

4Matching: building and reviewing the lineup

Approving the criteria before the search means the agent's later reasoning can be checked against something the CM already agreed to. That is the recruiting pattern (LinkedIn, Ashby).

5Production: one deliverable's life

Decision points on this diagram. Each state change is a decision point. Every object in the process (campaign, partnership, deliverable, payment) needs a diagram like this one, and each decision point needs the same four answers:

Decision point Who acts On what Possible outcomes What it changes next
Draft overdue System, then CM The creator and the deadline Nudge again · escalate · extend · swap Slack before go-live; whether the backup is used
Draft checked System The draft against the brief Pass · fix request · needs a person Whether the CM sees it at all
CM review CM The draft Approve · approve with changes · decline unless · decline Brand review, or another revision round
Brand review Client The draft Approve · feedback Go-live date
Post live System The live post Verified · fix request Payment

Most decision points have a small, fixed set of outcomes. Some, like building a lineup or splitting a budget, have many, and need a composing interface rather than a set of buttons. (Design position. It borrows two ideas from actor-network theory: people and systems are both actors, and a stable process can be treated as a black box, which is what a workflow contract is. ANT itself is backward-looking: it traces how a network came to be. Here the record is that trace, and the forward-looking part, using the reconstructed network to recommend what comes next, is an extension, not the theory.)

Draft review is exceptions first (Interview: the CPO wants a summary of findings that can go straight to the creator, not a confirmation of every check). The agent shows what failed with evidence and a drafted fix request; the CM edits or sends it. Passing checks collapse to a count. Creative judgment, and anything the checker can't assess, still goes to CM review. Products that aren't shipped skip the product-received fact upstream.

6Hard moment: a creator goes quiet with a deadline close

What makes this "hard" rather than a cron job: the escalation threshold depends on slack, substitutability, and history. The agent's job is to reduce the decision to a call with the context already loaded, and to keep the client informed only when it actually matters.

7In-flight optimization and budget pacing

This replaces the original "tranche" idea. Creator fees are mostly committed at contracting, so the levers are the reserve, the next wave, and swaps.

Micro-experiments (R2+). Before the main boost, the agent can spend a small capped test budget across audiences or placements, then propose splitting the rest of the reserve across 2–5 buckets with expected outcomes. The CM approves the split, and the same thresholds apply. See principles §10.

8The CM's day: from signals to the decision queue

Rejections with reasons are the agent's training signal, and the record of how autonomy is earned. This is the factory's tune the robot loop (Interview): when the same correction repeats, the agent proposes a policy change for the CM to accept, so one decision fixes the next twenty. Nothing in this loop is visible to the brand.

9Order-independent updates and conflicts

Facts arrive in any order up to a milestone, and the same update may be repeated by different people as part of overlapping batches. Principles are in discovery/framing.md §6.

Assumptions

What is public, what is invented, what is a design call

Source: docs/assumptions.md

Each entry carries one label. Public: published by Later or the industry (sources in discovery/research.md). Interview: said by Later leadership (CPO, CTO) during the interview process, paraphrased. Invented: made up for the exercise, or a design decision. The brief asks for assumptions to be written down, so add them as they are made.

These inputs are thin: three conversations and public material, with no time spent with a Services team. Treat everything here as an informed direction to validate, not a finding.

About Later and the Services team

  • Later Services runs fully managed campaigns for enterprise brands, from strategy through sourcing, production and reporting. Kickoff to first post takes 2–4 weeks. Public.
  • Work is done by account pods: Account Manager, Coordinators/Co-Ops, Talent Partnerships, with Strategy, Paid Media and Creative alongside. Public (job postings).
  • Later Influence is the system of record for campaigns, with a 12-stage creator workflow (13 named stages including Dropped, in four phases), draft review states, and stage-triggered automations. The agent operates on top of it. Public; integration invented.
  • Later EdgeAI already provides performance forecasts and creator-mix recommendations, and the agent consumes them rather than reinventing them. Public product; usage invented.
  • Creator intake (sign-ups, the Later Creator app) belongs to Creator Operations, not Services. Public (job postings).

What Later leadership said (interview)

  • Later sells outcomes, not software. The Services team is now the biggest internal customer of Later's own tools, and the product includes the delivery experience, timeline and client delight, not just the UI. Interview (CPO).
  • The direction is an agentic factory: agents do the work; people observe, govern, and tune the robot when something goes wrong. Separately, brands get a polished, front-desk experience and don't see the machinery. Today's tools mix the two, like an airport where travellers can see the baggage belts. Interview (CPO, CTO).
  • Each job moves along a maturity curve: manual → software-supported → AI-assisted (the human drives) → agentic (the agent starts, the human curates, governs and applies judgment and taste). Parts can go live before the whole is finished. Interview (CPO, CTO).
  • The planned architecture has a conductor agent that works in two modes: design time (design the campaign workflow; the output is a "bill of materials") and run time (execute that bill of materials). Interview (CTO).
  • The largest cost is cost to serve. The longer-term ambition is to make influencer marketing an agentic, programmatic channel, the way ad buying went programmatic. Efficiency matters, but so does the quality of outcomes. Interview (CPO).
  • Later sees itself as strong at producing results and weaker at making agentic interfaces intuitive, both for internal users and for clients. Interview (CTO).
  • A draft-review checker already exists as an internal prototype: it extracts content requirements from the campaign brief and checks the video and caption against them before the brand sees the draft. The CPO's view is that daily use should summarize the findings and let the reviewer send them to the creator, rather than ask the reviewer to confirm every check. Testing whether the checks are correct is a different job from designing daily use. Interview (CPO); the agent consuming it is invented.
  • The creator workflow has many variants: product shipped or not; awareness or sales objectives. Interview (CPO).
  • Matching criteria include brand suitability, brand safety, and the polish (production quality) of a creator's content. Interview (CTO).
  • Creators are businesses but behave like consumers, so creator-facing touchpoints need consumer-grade care. Interview (CTO).
  • A recurring UX gap is action hierarchy and labeling: the same label means different things on different screens, and secondary actions are styled as primary ones. Interview (CPO).

About the campaign manager

  • "Campaign manager" in the brief corresponds to Later's Account Manager: owns a portfolio of client accounts and is measured on delivery, client trust, renewal and expansion. Public (job postings); the mapping is ours.
  • A CM runs about 4–6 accounts with 2–4 live campaigns each. Invented.
  • A campaign has anywhere from about 10 to 100+ creators. Interview (the CTO used a 100-creator campaign as the typical example); Public (Premier Protein: 78). The demo uses small campaigns for legibility, so the queue and batch patterns have to hold at 100.
  • The CM already supervises human coordinators, but supervising an agent is a different skill with different tools; the agent is a participant in the pod, not a member. Design position; earlier drafts assumed the two were alike.

About campaigns and money

  • Creator fees are mostly committed at contracting (flat fee or 50/50). Mid-flight flexibility comes from an amplification reserve, the next wave, and swaps, not from released tranches. Industry practice; Later's specifics not public.
  • Each campaign holds a 20% paid amplification reserve. Spending more than $5k of it, or more than 25%, needs client approval. Invented.
  • Production workback: brief T-14, draft T-7, approval T-3, live T-0. Public (Later blog).
  • Whitelisting/Spark rights are negotiated per creator and may be missing or expiring. Industry practice.
  • Objectives span the full funnel: awareness and engagement (Later Influence heritage) through affiliate sales (Later Creator, formerly Mavely). Public; Interview (CPO).

About the agent

  • The agent acts alone on logistics (reminders, receipt tracking, post verification, internal status), proposes judgment calls, and never speaks to the client or commits money without approval in v1. Gated action types graduate to automatic as the record shows they're safe, within code-enforced limits, so escalations stay rare enough to be read. Design decision.
  • Autonomy is set per action type and can be raised or revoked. Design decision.
  • The reminder cadence before a draft deadline is T-10, T-8 and T-7. The agent escalates when the draft is late and slack to go-live falls below a threshold that depends on the creator's history and whether a backup is available. Invented.
  • Every proposal carries per-criterion evidence and a confidence level, not a single opaque score. Design decision.
  • The agent consumes the existing draft-review checker and shows exceptions first: what failed, with evidence, and a ready-to-send fix request to the creator. Passing checks collapse. Design decision, following the CPO's view above.
  • A CM's rejection with a reason is a tuning signal: repeated corrections of the same kind propose a policy change (for example, escalate this account earlier) instead of being fixed one at a time. Design decision.
  • Behaviour the agent repeats reliably should harden into rules or code over time, leaving the agent the cases that are hard to write rules for. The prototype's AGENT_POLICY is that rules layer. Design decision.
  • Several people and the agent update the same campaign concurrently, often through overlapping batches. Updates are keyed and idempotent, milestones gate on sets of facts rather than sequences, items can be added before they're complete (they show their gaps), equivalent inputs produce equivalent records, and stale writes become conflict cards rather than overwrites. Design decision.

About rollout and the interface

  • Later Influence is in production and account pods depend on it every day, so the agent ships as additive improvements to existing workflows, at a pace users can absorb, not as a replacement product. Design decision.
  • People, rules and the agent produce the same record for the same action; only the attribution differs. Anti-spam limits are enforced on that shared record. Design decision.
  • Repetitive work moves from agent to deterministic rules over time: roughly 80% rules, 10–15% agent, 5–10% escalated to a person. Invented, illustrative.
  • Most approvals reduce to four outcomes: approve, approve with conditions, decline unless, decline. Design decision; mirrors Later's public draft review states.
  • Chat is reachable from a command palette but is never the main way to work; forms and direct review stay primary. Design decision.
  • A right-hand rail (about 25% of the width) shows 2–4 widgets chosen from a fixed library by context. Each widget handles its own layout and data-quantity breakpoints (0, 1, ~10, ~100, 1,000+). Design decision.
  • Agent-drafted creator briefs and feedback can cut revision rounds from roughly 5–6 to 2–3. Invented hypothesis; the first thing to measure.
  • The agent can spend a small, capped test budget (about 5–10% of the amplification reserve) on early micro-experiments across audiences and placements, then propose splitting the remaining spend across 2–5 buckets for a person to approve. Invented; requires whitelisting rights.
  • The app is organized around four core objects (portfolio › account › campaign › asset) plus a cross-cutting roster. A campaign is a set of asset slots, often over-cast by 20–40% so there are backups. Invented; see information-architecture.md.
  • Layout: top navigation with a visible Find or ask entry point, a core context area of ~70–75% for the work, and a contextual widget rail of ~25–30%. Design decision.
  • During the move to top navigation, the prototype keeps a collapsible left sidebar as secondary navigation (accounts with open-proposal counts, Roster, and Activity). It may go once Find or ask and the portfolio view cover those jumps. Invented.
  • In the pod's Activity view, every entry names its actor: system (a rule), agent, or a person by name. The detailed trace (version, prompt, and inputs) sits behind that. Messages outside the pod always come from the account manager's team. Design decision; revised from person-or-system so the record matches the "one record, any actor" slide.
  • Agent output reaches people in four named classes: actions taken, drafts and proposals, information requests, and summaries in context. Every card names its class. A draft opens in an editable sheet and goes out only when a person presses Send; the send is one permanent entry from the account manager's team, and closing the sheet records nothing. Design decision; see design-system/02-ai/output-classes.md.
  • An information request states the assumption the agent made and what changes if it's wrong. The demo's example (Ana's audience assumed mostly US from her profile; her manager at "Northstar Talent" can confirm) is Invented. The agent-drafted creator brief exists as a fixture but isn't wired to a button, because briefs go out after the client approves the lineup, which the prototype doesn't model. Invented; a deliberate cut.
  • Undo follows what an action touched: internal (status, pacing, logging) can be undone; external (anything sent to a creator or client, including a change agreed on a call) and spend are permanent, and a correction is a new entry. Design decision; the effect taxonomy is Invented.
  • The data model (portfolio › account › campaign › asset, a campaign as slots and candidates) is organized the way I would organize it, not the way Later's data is known to be. It's the assumption I'm least comfortable with. A heuristic review of the current product and a working session with engineering come before any real proposal. Invented; highest-risk assumption.
  • Most decisions fit four outcomes. Two, three or five are fine in the same pattern; a decision with dozens of possible outcomes needs a different interface. Design decision; to test.
  • No user research, heuristic review or competitive teardown was done. Low-hanging improvements to the current UI are therefore not proposed. Constraint of the timebox.

Prior writing (Joshua Hall, Medium)

The author's own published positions that this work builds on.

  • Chat Is an Input, Not an Interface (June 2026). Conversation suits genuinely unstructured input. Forms, grids and direct manipulation stay better for most work, so the LLM should sit behind those surfaces. This grounds A9 and the decision to keep chat as a scoped ask affordance. https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/chat-is-an-input-not-an-interface-2686ba8b542d
  • Generative UI Is Three Things. Only One Ships. (May 2026). Of fully generated code, model-assembled layouts, and a fixed shell with a bounded area of dynamically chosen widgets, only the last is reliable enough to ship. This grounds A7: a curated widget library selected by state, not UI the model invents. https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/generative-ui-is-three-things-only-one-ships-136478c1a67b
  • CFS: Scoring Features Before You Argue About Them (June 2026). Rate Commonality × Frequency × Severity on a 1–5 scale and record the score with the decision. It is used here to rank which agent tasks to take on first and to justify the cuts. https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/cfs-scoring-features-before-you-argue-about-them-efa239a4ab74
  • Idempotent Design: When Order Shouldn't Matter (June 2026). Independent actions should give the same result in any order. Real sequencing constraints are separated from "shadow constraints" that the data model imposes. This grounds §6 of the framing: milestones that gate on sets, keyed idempotent updates, and conflict cards for overlapping work across the pod. https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/idempotent-design-when-order-shouldnt-matter-ca940b4cbf83
  • Owning the Design System (June 2026). A design system works only as a permanent, owned function measured by adoption and downstream value. This answers the brief's question of which patterns to standardize if this agent is one of several, and who owns them. https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/owning-the-design-system-8a28ea61372b
